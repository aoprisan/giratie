Wait counts the time a vehicle spends queued beyond the map or below walking pace on it. The score is the share of arriving traffic that gets through, times 30 / (30 + average wait in seconds): 100 means nobody waits and nothing piles up, a 30 s wait halves it. The grade uses the level-of-service bands for roundabout delay: A up to 10 s, B 15, C 25, D 35, E 50, F beyond.

What this models, and what it does not

Each car follows the one ahead with the Intelligent Driver Model. On give-way, a car enters when the ring is clear to its left. On signals, each approach has an entry light, a stop line on the ring just before it, and, on the arms that have one, a signalised pedestrian crossing a short way out with lights for both directions. Str. Andrei Șaguna has two more crossings further out, each with its own push button. The lights stand where the city’s own traffic model shows them, and the map draws them the way that model does: each stop line in the colour of its light. A queue held at an exit crossing can reach back across the ring, which is how the articulated buses blocked the Dumbrăvii direction on the morning described in the article.

The two roundabouts are the ones in the city’s traffic model: Piața Unirii × Bd. Corneliu Coposu × Str. Andrei Șaguna × Str. Emil Cioran (“Ramada”), and Bd. Vasile Milea × Calea Dumbrăvii × Piața Unirii × Str. Constantin Noica, the street that passes under the “blocul-plombă”. The city also named a third, Șos. Alba Iulia × Str. Morilor, but its model does not show it, so it is left out. The map is traced from the city’s model (“Fluxuri simultane”), at true scale and turned so the corridor runs left to right: each arm has its own entry and exit roads where the model draws them, Str. Andrei Șaguna comes in on its curved carriageway and leaves on the straight one, and two right-turn bypasses (Coposu to Șaguna, Dumbrăvii to V. Milea) let traffic skip the ring. The Coposu bypass waits for the Coposu entry light, as in the model.

Adaptive control works the way the city described it: detectors in each approach keep a green running while traffic keeps arriving, cut it after a gap of 2.5 s, skip approaches with nobody waiting, and give an approaching bus its green early. A green also ends early when the ring is half full. Pedestrian windows open only when someone is waiting.

Rings and approaches have two lanes where the city’s model draws them. Drivers pick a lane for where they are going: the right lane to turn right, the left to turn left, either to go straight on. Traffic from the right lane circles in the outer ring lane, from the left lane in the inner one; leaving from the inner lane means crossing the outer one, so those drivers give way. The city blamed most of the 6 October queues on drivers in the wrong lane; the “Drivers in the wrong lane” slider tests that. Those drivers notice late and stop before the stop line to squeeze across, or cut across inside the ring, stopping there if they must, and show in pink. In this model even 30% of drivers in the wrong lane add at most about 15% to trip times under signals and nothing under give-way: the choice between fixed-time and adaptive signals matters far more. The second slider makes some of them force their way instead: they only notice inside the ring and cut straight across it at the last moment, straddling both lanes so traffic behind them in either lane has to stop. That does hurt where the signals are already saturated, with fixed-time signals on both roundabouts at a busy peak up to a third slower, but adaptive signals absorb most of it and give-way none of it. Wrong lanes make a signal problem worse; in this model they do not create one.

On 6 October the lanes had no markings at all, and the city said the signals would come back once they were repainted. The “Lane markings: none” setting models that: drivers take either lane, find out inside the ring and cut across, and do not drive side by side. It costs adaptive signals half as much time again or more (both roundabouts: about 2 minutes a trip becomes about 3, more at a busy peak) and give-way almost nothing. Missing markings explain a good part of that morning; even with them painted, the signals here stay slower than give-way. Traffic volumes and signal timings are assumptions, so read the result as a direction, never as a forecast.

Traffic is mixed: besides cars of different lengths there are vans, lorries and articulated buses, and every driver has their own preferred speed, following distance, acceleration and the gaps they will accept. Heavy vehicles pull away slowly from a stop line, which costs the signals green time, up to twice the trip time with adaptive signals on both roundabouts, while give-way hardly notices. Set “Vehicles” to identical cars to compare. The demand slider goes up to 300%: give-way then carries about 3,000 vehicles an hour, adaptive signals about 1,900 and fixed-time about 1,200, and the rest queue beyond the map.
